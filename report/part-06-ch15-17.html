Chapter 16

KPI & Metrics Reference

The six headline KPIs documented in full, followed by a complete reference table covering every remaining metric in the interface.

16.1 The six headline KPIs

Total Active Power

Definition
The rate at which the site is consuming useful electrical energy at this instant.
Simple
How fast the whole site is using electricity right now — the speedometer, not the odometer.
Technical
Real power: the component of supplied power performing actual work, as opposed to reactive power which circulates without doing work.
Unit
MW on the tile; kW in the sub-line. 1 MW = 1,000 kW.
Purpose
The single most important operational number — the basis of capacity and cost.
Why included
Every other consumption figure is a subdivision of this one.
Data source
Sum of smoothed readings across all monitored non-generation points.IMPL
Calculation
site.kw = Σ smoothed kW of all load points
Example
4,526 kW displays as 4.53 MW.
Interpretation
Typical 3,000–5,500 kW depending on hour. Judge against typical for this hour, not against a fixed threshold — 4,500 kW is normal at 20:00 and abnormal at 04:00.
Relationships
Integrated over time gives Energy. Divided by voltage and power factor gives Current. Combined with reactive power gives apparent power.

Reactive Power

Definition
Power that oscillates between source and load without performing net work.
Simple
Electricity that sloshes back and forth. Motors and transformers need it to build magnetic fields, but it does no useful work and still occupies cable capacity.
Technical
The imaginary component of complex power, arising from the phase displacement between voltage and current in inductive loads.
Unit
MVAR on the tile (megavolt-ampere reactive); kVAr per substation.
Purpose
High reactive power means cables and transformers carry current that produces nothing, and utilities commonly penalise it.
Data source
Derived from real power and power factor per substation, then summed.IMPL
Calculation
Q = P × tan(arccos(PF)) per substation; site total is the sum.
Example
P = 3,319 kW at PF 0.95 gives Q = 3,319 × tan(arccos 0.95) = 1,091 kVAr.
Interpretation
Rises as power factor falls. Typically 25–35% of real power on a motor-heavy site. A sharp rise with no corresponding rise in real power indicates increased inductive load or failing power-factor correction.
Relationships
Directly determined by power factor; combines with real power to give apparent power.

Power Factor

Definition
The ratio of real power to apparent power — how efficiently supplied power is converted into useful work.
Simple
A score from 0 to 1. 1.0 means every unit supplied does useful work. 0.8 means a fifth of the capacity is wasted moving reactive power around.
Technical
The cosine of the phase angle between voltage and current waveforms.
Unit
Dimensionless, 0 to 1. Displayed to three decimal places.
Purpose
Improving power factor is frequently the cheapest available saving on an industrial site.
Data source
Computed site-wide from real and apparent power.IMPL
Calculation
PF = P ÷ S where S = √(P² + Q²)
Example
P = 4,526 kW, Q = 1,420 kVAr gives S = 4,744 kVA and PF = 0.954.
Interpretation
Above 0.95 good. 0.90–0.95 acceptable. Below 0.90 usually attracts a penalty and the tile shows a warning. Falls as motor load rises — a normal daily variation, not a fault.
Relationships
Governs reactive power and current. For the same real power, a lower power factor means more current and higher losses.

Frequency

Definition
The rate at which alternating current reverses direction.
Simple
How many times per second the electricity changes direction. It must stay very close to 50 — it is a live indicator of whether national generation is keeping up with national demand.
Technical
The fundamental frequency of the supply waveform, determined by the rotational speed of synchronous generators across the interconnected grid.
Unit
Hz (hertz), two decimal places.
Purpose
Sensitive equipment and motor speeds depend on it; sustained excursion signals grid instability.
Data source
Modelled as a slow wander about 50 Hz, identical at every bus because frequency is a system-wide quantity.IMPL
Calculation
f = clamp(50 + 0.045·sin(φ) + noise, 49.7, 50.3)
Interpretation
Statutory band 49.5–50.5 Hz. Below 49.5 means demand exceeds generation nationally; above 50.5 the reverse. A site cannot influence this, only respond to it.DOMAIN
Relationships
Independent of site load. Unlike every other KPI, it reports on the grid rather than on the site.

Average Voltage (L-L)

Definition
Mean line-to-line voltage across the three substations.
Simple
The electrical "pressure" pushing current through the wires. Too low and motors overheat; too high and insulation degrades.
Technical
RMS potential difference between any two of the three phase conductors, averaged across the phases and then across the substations.
Unit
kV (kilovolts), two decimal places. Nominal 11 kV.
Purpose
Out-of-band voltage shortens equipment life without ever causing an outage.
Data source
Derived per bus with a load-dependent droop and a small independent per-phase offset, then averaged.IMPL
Calculation
Vphase = 11 × (1 − loading×0.028 + imbalance + noise)
Example
Phases at 10.962, 10.905 and 10.875 kV average to 10.91 kV — within band.
Interpretation
Band 10.5–11.5 kV (±4.5%). Voltage falling as load rises is normal droop. Voltage below band at high load indicates an undersized or overloaded supply path.
Relationships
Falls as loading rises; determines current for a given power; underpins the phase-imbalance calculation.

Energy Today

Definition
Total electrical energy consumed since monitoring began.
Simple
The odometer to Total Active Power's speedometer. This is what an electricity bill charges for.
Technical
The time integral of real power over the measurement period.
Unit
kWh below 1,000, MWh above. 1 MWh = 1,000 kWh.
Purpose
The billing quantity and the basis of any consumption-reduction target.
Data source
Accumulated on every ingest from smoothed power and elapsed time.IMPL
Calculation
E += P × Δt ÷ 3,600,000 with Δt in milliseconds, rejecting gaps over 60 s.
Example
4,500 kW sustained for 2 seconds adds 4,500 × 2,000 ÷ 3,600,000 = 2.5 kWh.
Interpretation
Monotonically increasing. Its rate of increase is average demand. Judge against the expected-for-elapsed reference on the gauge, not in isolation.
Relationships
Power × time. Divided by elapsed hours gives average demand; with peak demand gives load factor; with the emission factor gives CO₂.

Chapter 17

Technical Terminology

The terms and abbreviations appearing in the interface, each explained first in plain language and then technically. Only terms actually present in the dashboard are included.

17.1 Power and energy terms

kW — kilowatt

Simple: a measure of how fast electricity is being used. A domestic kettle draws about 2 kW. Technical: one thousand watts; the SI unit of power, being energy transfer per unit time. Unit of: power. Measured: in this implementation, reported per point by the feed and summed. Why it matters: it is the fundamental quantity every consumption figure is built from. Interpretation: a rate, not a quantity — meaningful only at an instant. Example: a tube well pump drawing 15 kW.

MW — megawatt

Simple: one million watts, used because site totals in kW become unwieldy. Technical: 1 MW = 1,000 kW. Why it appears: the KPI tiles display site totals in MW for legibility while retaining kW in the sub-line. Example: 4,526 kW shown as 4.53 MW.

kWh — kilowatt-hour

Simple: the amount of electricity used, as opposed to how fast. One kilowatt sustained for one hour. Technical: a unit of energy equal to 3.6 megajoules. Formula: E (kWh) = P (kW) × t (h). Why it matters: this is the billing unit. Interpretation: always increasing; its rate of increase is average power. Example: a 10 kW pump running 5 hours consumes 50 kWh.

MWh — megawatt-hour

1,000 kWh. The dashboard switches automatically from kWh to MWh above 1,000 kWh to keep the displayed figure readable.IMPL

kVA and MVA — kilovolt-ampere

Simple: the total electrical load a cable or transformer must physically carry, including the part that does no useful work. Technical: apparent power, the vector sum of real and reactive power. Formula: S = √(P² + Q²). Why it appears: transformers are rated in kVA, so all capacity figures in the register are in kVA. Example: the site's 109 transformers total 18,530 kVA.

kVAr and MVAR — reactive volt-ampere

Simple: the portion of power that does no work but is still required by motors and transformers. Technical: the imaginary component of complex power. Formula: Q = P × tan(arccos PF). Interpretation: the lower the power factor, the higher this is for the same useful output.

17.2 Supply quality terms

V, kV — volt, kilovolt

Simple: electrical pressure. Technical: potential difference. In this system: 11 kV high-tension distribution — evidenced by the register's "VCBs HT 11 KV" entry — stepped down to 400 V for buildings.IMPL

L-L — line-to-line

Voltage measured between two phase conductors rather than between one phase and earth. The standard way to quote three-phase voltage, which is why the dashboard labels its voltage figures "L-L".

A — ampere

Simple: how much electricity is flowing. Technical: the SI unit of current. Formula used: I = P ÷ (√3 · V · PF) for a three-phase system. Why it matters: cables are sized by current; exceeding their rating overheats them.

Hz — hertz

Simple: cycles per second. Technical: the supply's fundamental frequency, nominally 50 Hz in this region. Interpretation: a grid-level quantity that a site observes rather than controls.

PF — power factor

Covered fully in Chapter 16. In brief: the ratio of useful power to total supplied power, between 0 and 1.

R-Y, Y-B, B-R — phase pairs

Simple: industrial supply uses three live conductors, coloured Red, Yellow and Blue. These three labels are the three possible pairings between them. Technical: the three line-to-line voltage measurements of a three-phase system. Why they appear: the voltage profile plots all three so imbalance between them is visible.

Phase imbalance

Simple: the three phases carrying noticeably different loads. Formula: maximum phase deviation from the mean, divided by the mean. Why it matters: sustained imbalance overheats and destroys three-phase motors. Interpretation: below 2% acceptable.

17.3 Network terms

HT / LT — high tension / low tension

High-voltage distribution (11 kV here) and low-voltage final distribution (400 V). Electricity travels at high voltage because losses fall as voltage rises for the same power. The register records 38 km of HT and 108 km of LT cable.IMPL

Substation

Where high voltage is received and stepped down for local distribution. Three exist: PNS Hafeez, ARC and NHQ, plus 13 satellite stations serving outstations.

Transformer

Simple: the device that changes voltage from high to low. Technical: a static machine coupling two windings by a magnetic field. Rated in: kVA. Example: a 200 kVA unit carries roughly 180 kW of real load at 0.9 power factor.

Feeder

A cable circuit carrying power from a substation to a group of loads. The register states three feeders; the dashboard models one per substation.

Busbar

The heavy conductor inside a substation to which everything connects — the common junction between incoming supply and outgoing feeders. Drawn as the horizontal line on the single-line diagram.

VCB — vacuum circuit breaker

Simple: an automatic switch that disconnects a circuit when something goes wrong. Technical: a breaker interrupting current within a vacuum chamber, which extinguishes the arc cleanly; standard at medium voltage. In this system: eight units at 11 kV across four register-named sites.IMPL

DB — distribution board

The panel of protective devices inside a building splitting incoming supply into final circuits — a domestic consumer unit, scaled up. The register records 475 with no further detail.

SLD — single-line diagram

The standard engineering drawing of an electrical network, representing each three-phase circuit as one line. Universally read by electrical engineers, which is why the dashboard uses this form rather than a generic chart.

Solar PV / kWp

Photovoltaic panels generating electricity from sunlight. kWp is "kilowatt peak" — output under full reference sun. The register states 13 systems but gives no ratings.IMPL

17.4 Load and capacity terms

Load

Any device or group of devices consuming electricity; also the amount consumed. The dashboard uses it in both senses — "load group" (the devices) and "site load" (the amount).

Demand

Power drawn at a given moment. Essentially interchangeable with load in this context.

Peak demand

The highest demand reached in a period. Commercially significant because utilities frequently levy a charge based on it, independent of total energy.DOMAIN

Connected load vs demand

Connected load is the total that could be switched on at once; demand is what is actually drawn. Connected capacity here is 16.7 MW while typical demand is around 4.5 MW.

Diversity

The reason for that gap. Across 2,157 buildings, not everything runs simultaneously, so the real total sits far below the theoretical maximum. This is why networks are not sized to connected load.DOMAIN

Capacity / firm capacity

The load the network can carry safely and continuously, derived here from installed transformer kVA at an assumed 0.9 power factor.

Utilisation

Load as a percentage of capacity. Distribution transformers typically run at 30–60%.

Load profile

The characteristic shape of demand across a day. Residential load peaks in the evening; office load at midday; street lighting runs only at night. Each asset group in the register carries its own profile.IMPL

Generation

Electricity produced on site rather than imported. Here this means solar only — the register records no generators of any kind.IMPL

17.5 Monitoring and alarm terms

SCADA

Full form: Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition. Simple: the standard family of industrial systems that collect readings from field equipment and present them centrally. In this system: the dashboard is designed to sit on top of one; the reference server emulates its data interface.

Tag / Point ID

The unique code identifying one monitored location, such as TW-01 or MED-HOSP. Everything keys off it: readings, limits, alarms and history.

Telemetry

Measurements transmitted automatically from remote equipment, as opposed to manual reading.

SSE — server-sent events

A one-way streaming protocol over HTTP in which the server pushes updates as they occur. Used in preference to polling because it is lower-latency and lighter on both ends.

REST polling

The alternative: the client requests the current readings on a fixed interval. Simpler for a gateway to implement, at the cost of latency.

EMA — exponential moving average

Simple: a smoothing method weighting recent readings more heavily than older ones. Formula: α = 1 − e−Δt/τ, then value = previous + α(new − previous), with τ = 6 s. Why it matters: raw sensor jitter would otherwise read as plant instability.IMPL

Data quality states

Deliberately a separate axis from severity. Good — fresh sample. Pending — not heard from yet at start-up, explicitly not an alarm. Stale — over 15 s old. Offline — over 40 s old; raises a communications alarm. Bad — the meter reported bad quality, and limits are not evaluated on it.IMPL
